## Featured: AntiCam

**Role and dates:** Founder, Tully Tech — sole designer, builder and author of every AntiCam document
`[cv; deck.js#L8]`. Called two years of work in both the 2025 Mastery plan and the Fall 2026 deck, but
the earliest dated artifact is the June 7, 2023 doorbell test, so on the deck's own evidence it is
three or more years old `[mastery#page-1; deck.js#L446; cb-15]`.

**One line (first person):** "AntiCam is my wearable privacy device — an infrared LED array and a
retro-reflective lining that keep cameras from capturing a usable image of me, without jamming or
damaging anything." `[outline#L3; cv]`

**Facts**

- Most cameras use night-vision-capable sensors that respond to near-infrared light the eye cannot
  see; AntiCam works in that gap `[deck.js#L192-196]`.
- A high-output IR LED array projects invisible light at any lens facing the wearer; auto-exposure
  cannot compensate, so the wearer washes out into glare while the camera keeps recording
  `[deck.js#L192-196, #L235]`.
- A light sensor spikes the array when it detects a flash, and a retro-reflective lining returns the
  flash toward its source — the second defense `[deck.js#L192-196; mastery#page-1]`.
- Through a phone camera the emitter reads as a magenta glow; to the naked eye it is dark
  `[deck.js#L210; cb-09]`. Cap V2 puts the emitters under the brim with the lining behind them
  `[deck.js#L212; cb-17]`.
- Three planned formats share one emitter, driver and battery platform: clip-on Pin $30–45, Cap $40–65
  (360°, lining, flash detection), Room Unit $100+ (no prototype photo exists) `[deck.js#L403-405]`.
- It emits infrared light only — no radio interference, no damage to cameras or networks; the deck
  frames this as legal by design, light not radio, outside the FCC's jammer rules
  `[deck.js#L502, #L524]`.
- Stated limits in Alex's own materials: weaker in bright daylight; not for evading traffic or
  law-enforcement cameras; not for restricted areas; intent stated at checkout and in manufacturing
  partnerships `[deck.js#L525-526]`.
- Watched risks: daylight performance, IR-cut filters, copycats. The stated edge is speed and design,
  **not a patent — none exists** `[deck.js#L504]`.
- Positioning: RF jammers are active but illegal, detectors find lenses without blocking them,
  IR-blocking eyewear covers only the face, adversarial apparel depends on the algorithm
  `[deck.js#L475, #L485-489]`.
- Tested against a live Ring doorbell camera in night mode — same camera, same street, a person in the
  pin prototype walking up the steps; the sensor records a glowing orb where the face should be
  `[deck.js#L235-236; cb-15]`.
- The current device is hand-built; a custom PCB, battery-safety testing and **IR eye-safety testing
  are planned, not done** `[deck.js#L581]`.
- Earlier prototypes: laser-cut wood/MDF and acrylic plates, 3D-printed cases, hand-soldered coin-cell
  packs joined with copper tape, a 10W-class COB LED with a 3×3 emitter grid; the Tully Tech logo is
  laser-engraved into lids from the second prototype on `[cb-10; cb-11; cb-12; cb-13; cb-14]`.
- Audience priority: short-term-rental travelers (NOW), parents and creators/public figures (NEXT),
  journalists and activists, hosts/hotels/venues, executive protection (LATER)
  `[deck.js#L308-313; mastery#page-2]`.

## Featured: Low-cost prosthetic arm

**Role and dates:** Research Assistant, Stevens Institute of Technology, summer 2024 — designed and
fabricated a prosthetic arm under faculty supervision and researched prosthetics design, construction,
costs and the physics of human motion `[cv]`; continued afterward through Avenues' Mastery Program
`[root; essays]`. The 16-slide design brief names Alex as author but states no role title,
institution, supervisor or date `[brief p1]`.

**One line (first person):** "A 3D-printable lower-arm prosthetic — hand and forearm — built from
off-the-shelf parts, driven by servos through tendon-style cords, controlled by a myoelectric sensor,
and sized to the wearer from hand measurements." `[brief p5, p7, p8, p9, p12]`

**Facts**

- Scoped as a lower-arm prosthetic covering the hand and forearm `[brief p5]`.
- Requirements: off-the-shelf parts, most 3D-printable; modular, easy-to-assemble parts; myoelectric
  control; performs more than 80% of common tasks; and — listed last and called the most important —
  costs under $200 to produce `[brief p5]`.
- Four-stage process: Sketches/Ideation → CAD → Prototyping → Testing. The CAD is parametric from
  average hand sizing; mechanisms were prototyped separately before integration and put through
  strength, grip, actuation and functionality tests `[brief p6]`.
- Fingers use a tendon-inspired cord system: an elastic cord holds the finger upright, a Kevlar cord
  driven by a servo moves it; on the render the elastic path runs outside the bend, the Kevlar inside
  `[brief p7]`. A finger is six printed pieces plus two pin rods `[brief p7]`.
- The forearm houses five subsystems: servos, a wrist control system, an Arduino, batteries and a
  myoelectric control system `[brief p8]`.
- Control uses a specialised EMG (myoelectric) sensor that turns muscle-contraction signals into
  readable analog values and drives a movement response `[brief p9]`; per the private drafts, finger
  actuation was built first and sensors integrated afterward `[essays]`.
- The model is parametric on seven labeled hand measurements — hand circumference, hand length, palm
  length, wrist circumference, finger-root, interphalangeal and distal interphalangeal joint
  circumference — across Digits 1–5 `[brief p12]`.
- "Auto Sizing" runs user measurements into a neural network that outputs cell size and type, looping
  through iterations in nTop Automate `[brief p13]`; the résumé says "digitally-adaptable,
  customizable" and never mentions AI `[cv]`.
- The build is modular — hand, wrist collar and end plate break into separate printed segments, pins
  and joint pieces `[brief p14]` — but the brief ties modularity to **assembly and fabrication speed,
  not repair**; "modular for quick repair" comes from `[root]`/`[essays]` only `[brief p5, p15]`.
- Goals: make prosthetics more affordable, and show reliable prosthetics can be produced at lower cost
  while keeping critical functionality, using accessible materials `[brief p5]`; `[root]` adds
  partnering with manufacturers and organizations to reach underserved communities.
- Next steps in the brief, all future work: connect with mobile prosthetic outreach groups such as
  those run by the VA; interchangeable snap-together parts with remote 3D printing; patient access in
  developing nations and low-income neighborhoods `[brief p15]`.

## Featured: CeraPiper

**Role and dates:** Research Intern, "Cera Piper (Cornell Tech x Technion)", summer 2026 — worked on
CAD and slicing software for a custom ceramic printer `[cv]`. **No CeraPiper source document names
Alex or attributes any component to him:** the README's only institution is the Matter of Tech Lab and
the paper draft is bylined "Anonymous Author(s)"; the draft's reference list confirms he is **not** an

**One line (first person):** "I was a research intern on CeraPiper, a CAD tool for designing custom
hollow ceramic pipes that assemble into structures which cool a space by evaporating water."
`[README#about-the-project; cv]`

**Facts**

- Evaporative cooling with ceramics is a low-cost, energy-efficient alternative to air conditioning but
  has lacked accessible computational design tools; CeraPiper couples real-time control of extrusion
  geometry with a professional CAD environment `[README]`.
- Users compose whole assemblies — pipes, ribs, curves, branches, plug-and-socket connectors — as
  parametric features; the tool translates the design into machine instructions for a shape-shifting
  die on a commercial clay pug mill, and the segments assemble into chandelier-style cooling devices
  `[README]`.
- **Layer 1, design — Onshape / FeatureScript:** custom parametric features (ribbed straight and curved
  pipes, perpendicular branches, connectors, junction collars, linear pattern) sharing one library of
  fabrication constants; all profiles hexagonal, matching the die `[README#implementation]`.
- Three kinds of design-time constraint: **hard** (enumerated inner diameters, outer diameter 40–78 mm,
  rotation in 30° increments, length capped by the bed), **corrective** (a ribbed pipe snaps its length
  to whole rib increments and reports the adjustment, so extrusion never ends mid-rib) and **advisory**
  (an unsupported span beyond 150 mm turns the geometry red with a sag warning) `[README]`.
- **Layer 2, middleware — Python / Flask:** queries the Onshape feature tree through the REST API,
  rebuilds the assembly as a directed graph of fabrication primitives, traverses it depth-first to
  linearize each branch into a contiguous extrusion sequence, and splits long runs at the conveyor-bed
  limit and at perpendicular junctions finished by hand `[README]`.
- The result is serialized as a JSON **Function Stack** — a procedural recipe — rendered in a WebGL 3D
  preview and a 2D cross-section, and used to generate the printable bed blueprint `[README]`.
- **Layer 3, machine — Arduino (C++):** firmware maps primitives to motor steps and actuator outputs,
  regulates pug-mill feed rate, drives the die, and independently re-enforces mechanical thresholds as
  a safety layer `[README]`.
- **The paper blueprint** is the physical layer: a 20 cm-wide sheet printed on a standard plotter that
  runs along the bed beneath the freshly extruded clay, carrying which part is being extruded, where to
  cut, the mandrel size in use and where to hand-cut holes for perpendicular connectors — nine
  toggleable layers, per-bed part name, number and notes `[README#the-paper-blueprint]`.
- The CAM-to-CAD reformulation is the story: the earlier machine-facing interface was "controllable,
  but not designable" — makers reasoned about extrusion parameters in isolation and could not compose
  or evaluate the assembled object. Each machine parameter became an editable design element, so the
  assembled object, not the print sequence, is the primary representation `[README#from-cam-to-cad]`.
  The README's two-column table (die aperture travel → outer diameter; extrusion duration → length in
  mm; instruction order → derived from how parts attach; operator sag judgment → a 150 mm advisory)
  translates directly into a two-column block on the site.
- Interface: a tab bar splitting the design into fabricable beds (Main, branches, Perpendicular
  Connectors), a straight/curved 3D viewport, an inline-editable Components panel, the interactive bed
  blueprint `[README]`.
- Stack: Arduino Uno, Arduino IDE 2.3.3, ArduinoJson 7.4.2, Python 3, Flask 3.1.1, PySerial 3.5,
  Requests, NetworkX, python-dotenv, Waitress; p5.js (WEBGL + 2D) frontend `[README#prerequisites]`.

## Voice

Vocabulary and themes only — never reproduce essay sentences.

- **Verbs:** designed & fabricated; researched, designed & modeled; tested & refined; built & tested;
  iterate; prototype; hone; optimize; fabricate & test; recruit → nurture → grow → blossom.
- **Sequences:** research → purchase → build → test → prototype → produce → sell → advertise → publish;
  prototype in wood, finalize in metal; intake, sort, transfer, unload.
- **Camera verbs:** blocks / emits / reflects; floods / saturates / washes out / overwhelms / spikes /
  blinds.
- **AntiCam nouns:** IR LED array, near-infrared band, retro-reflective lining, night-vision-capable
  sensor, auto-exposure, coin cells, laser-cut housing, charging port, IR-cut filters.
- **Robot nouns:** hooded flywheel launcher, turret, adjustable hood, counter-rollers, sorting paddles,
  vectoring slots, centered transfer port, ballast weights, odometry pods, Limelight, IMU, Kalman
  filter, Task System, PEREGRINE, auton, master sketch, GT2 pulleys, HTD5 belts, TPU grips.
- **Prosthetic nouns:** myoelectric interface, tendon-inspired cord system, elastic and Kevlar cord,
  Digit Design, Auto Sizing, parametric design, modular design.
- **CeraPiper nouns:** Function Stack, FeatureScript, shape-shifting die, pug mill, mandrel, conveyor
  bed, paper blueprint, fabricable tabs, hard / corrective / advisory constraints.
- User control over when one is seen, not concealment; "a zone, not a kill switch".
- Harmless by physics — light, not radio; physics, not patterns.
- Honest about limits — states weaknesses unprompted, e.g. weaker in daylight.
- Quantify everything: iteration counts, percentages, milliseconds, before/after numbers.
- Iteration framed as V1/V2/V3, each with a measured improvement.
- Modularity and repairability as selling points in their own right.
- Regenerative, networked leadership — the hydra metaphor; seeds → roots → blossom for team growth.
- Outreach as scale plus touch; exposure → participation → leadership.
- Cost stated as a design constraint, always a ceiling or a comparison; prefers "accessible" and
  "affordable" to "disruptive" or "revolutionary".
- Leads with the person affected, then works back to the mechanism; problem in dollars and population
  counts first, solution as a requirements checklist second.
- Pairs a technical outcome with an ethics question — does it work, should it exist.
- Defines a product by what it refuses to do ("Unlike camera jammers…").
- Failure as informational feedback; constraints framed as help, not restriction.
- Biomimicry as a named design method; teaching and building treated as inseparable.
- Plain, unhyped register — concrete numbers, no adjectives of praise; one dry, self-deprecating aside
  at most.
