### 5.5 Robot detail: Monti / Monty (FTC #17253, Alex as Captain)

**Identity and naming**

- The robot is named MONTY, after the Monty Hall Problem, and its design is built around a 3-ball
  active intake capable of sorting artifacts
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10).
- Robot aesthetics derive from Triton, the team namesake: a wave-like body structure and blue
  colouring for water (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-14).

**Modular, screwless design — the headline claim**

- Alex's own portfolio page calls it the first fully modular FTC robot, designed to be easily
  repaired and disassembled within seconds without screws
  (content/source/portfolio/01-monti-robot.jpg#page-1).
- The team's engineering portfolio states the rule differently: following the team's "8 screw
  rule", each subsystem is removable in a maximum of 45 seconds
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-13).
- TODO(alex): "Screwless" (CLAUDE.md; content/source/portfolio/01-monti-robot.jpg#page-1) versus
  the documented "8 screw rule / 45 seconds"
  (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-13). These are not the same claim.
  Confirm which to publish; "few screws, 45-second subsystem swap" is the one with a measurement
  behind it.
- TODO(alex): Source or basis for "First fully modular FTC robot"
  (content/source/portfolio/01-monti-robot.jpg#page-1). Consider softening to "the first we know
  of" unless there is evidence.

**Prototyping and iteration**

- The team built the first iterations of the robot entirely from wooden plates for fast,
  inexpensive prototyping, then finalized the design in metal
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10).
- Using wood and the modular system the team went through 4 intake iterations, 3 launcher
  iterations and 2 transfer iterations before committing to metal
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-14).
- The design poster counts 5 full iterations of the robot overall
  (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-f.png).
- The team's stated design loop is Goals → Brainstorm → CAD → Test → Prototype → Optimize → Repeat,
  with the robot fully designed before build (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-f.png).
- CAD is done in Onshape on parametric master sketches, where one variable updates the whole
  design; the team says this speeds iteration by 50%
  (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-14).

**Chassis and ballast**

- The drivetrain is a modified GoBilda Strafer chassis with four GoBilda 435 RPM motors, chosen for
  simplicity, modularity and efficiency
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10).
- The team compared three chassis before choosing it: the swerve drive (fastest, very complex, not
  modular, developed in conjunction with another team), the Ball drive (a fully unique motion
  system developed by Tritonics — high torque and speed, oversized, not modular), and the standard
  GoBilda Strafer (modular, very simple, slower than the other two)
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10).
- Steel ballast blocks optimize the robot's center of mass for faster, more stable movement
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-13).
- Polycarbonate bumpers increase static and collision resistance
  (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-13).
- Ergonomic carry handles with TPU grips are placed over the center of mass, after the team
  struggled to lift the robot (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10).

**Custom pathing software (Alex's portfolio page framing)**

- Alex's portfolio page states that Monti uses custom-coded software, designed in collaboration
  with PhD researchers at the Simons Foundation, which mixes "infinite variable calculus" to
  calculate the most efficient path in any situation with an AI layer that makes live adjustments
  and commands during a match, in place of traditional pathing algorithms
  (content/source/portfolio/01-monti-robot.jpg#page-1).
- The team's own software page names that software **PEREGRINE** — see §5.8
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-6.png#P15).
- Monti's key innovations, as labelled on the portfolio page, are "Modularity and Three Ball Intake"
  (content/source/portfolio/01-monti-robot.jpg#page-1).

### 5.6 Subsystems (Monty, FTC #17253) — team work

The intake, transfer and launcher pages are team-authored and name no student, so everything in
this subsection is the team's work (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10).

**Intake**

- The sorting intake handles intaking, storage and sorting in one subsystem, and the team describes
  it as one of a kind out of roughly 4,000 teams surveyed
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-2.png#P11;
  content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-1.png#P10).
- Architecture: two counter-rollers rapidly intake artifacts from all three positions, then a third
  roller transfers them onward
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-2.png#P11).
- The 3-wide intake expands the intake area to 18 inches and takes in three artifacts
  simultaneously (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-2.png#P11;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-16).
- The design task was a large intake area plus sorting while maintaining rapid fire; the
  constraints were intake compression, rearrangement speed during sorting, ball jams, and intaking
  excess artifacts (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-2.png#P11).
- Stated benefits: artifact sorting, an ultra-wide 18 in intake, overintaking prevention, three
  artifacts at once. Stated costs: potentially slow unload speed and increased complexity, mitigated
  by an artifact queue in the transfer for a 100 ms unload rate and a modular intake for faster
  iterations (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-2.png#P11).
- Iteration history, as labelled on the page: the first version at Qualifier 1 used a 3-wide 18 in
  intake with a flat ramp that jammed and could not sort, plus an offset transfer port for simple
  ball queuing; V2 at Qualifier 3 added vectoring slots that guide and retain artifacts, but the
  flat ramp still caused inconsistent alignment and jams; V3 at Qualifier 9 moved to a centered port
  that shortened the transfer path and shortened the vectoring slots to reduce drag; V4 at Super
  Qualifier 2 added a colour-sensor array tracking each artifact's position inside the intake and
  sorting paddles that push artifacts into the transfer in the desired order and block excess balls
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-2.png#P11).
- Measured iteration results from the poster deck: V1 flat ramp, 85% intake rate and 7 s from
  intake to launcher; V2 vectoring slots and dual rail, 97% and 5 s; V3 centered transfer and
  rubber-band grip, 100% and 2 s (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-15).
- Rapid wooden (laser-cut plywood) prototyping was used to hone roller and ramp compression
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-2.png#P11;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-f.png).
- The intake was inspired by the intake mechanism of the Clearbot Robotics Alligator Robot, a
  garbage-collecting boat with a modular active roller intake that intakes, sorts and stores
  garbage (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-2.png#P11;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-f.png).
- The sorting example shown is a GPP → PPG rearrangement of the green/purple artifact pattern
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-2.png#P11;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-16).

**Transfer**

- The transfer pulls pre-sorted artifacts from the intake and stores them linearly to achieve a
  100 millisecond sorted transfer to the launcher — which the team says no other surveyed team has
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-3.png#P12).
- Design task: rapid, consistent transfer from storage to launcher, under constraints of limited
  internal volume, compressible artifacts, jam and double-feed risk, and velocity control for
  consistent launches (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-3.png#P12).
- Components: surgical-tubing rollers geared to 2000 rpm for a large intake area; a high-torque
  1000 rpm roller for multi-ball transfer; gecko-wheel rollers geared to 2000 rpm for grip; and a
  middle-ball blocker linkage that holds a ball for sorting and launching
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-3.png#P12).
- The transfer was modelled in the same Onshape part studio as the intake so the fit and
  compression match, and is entirely parametric for faster iterations
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-3.png#P12;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-f.png).
- Finite element analysis was used to reduce material while keeping strength, and dozens of trials
  tuned wheel torque and compression
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-3.png#P12;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-f.png).
- V1 → V2: V1 fed inconsistently from the intake, jammed when holding more than one ball, and took
  1.5 s to transfer to the launcher; V2 added 2 intaking counter-rollers, transfers 2 artifacts
  simultaneously, and transfers in 100 ms
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-3.png#P12;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-16).

**Launcher**

- The launcher is a hooded flywheel launcher mounted on a turret, with compression, inertia and
  launch angles optimized experimentally
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-4.png#P13;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-f.png).
- Design task: high throughput, accurate far-zone cycles and fast aiming, under constraints of
  speed loss from friction and artifact compression, packaging inside the turret's range, and a
  2-motor limit (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-4.png#P13).
- Launcher variables — compression, angle, wheel size — are defined on one master sketch so the
  team can iterate indefinitely
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-4.png#P13;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-f.png).
- Testing used Vernier Graphical Analysis to measure artifact exit velocity and set compression at
  8 mm; 200+ trials were analyzed to derive a launcher speed regression, **v = d · 2.64 + 1013**,
  predicting speed from shot distance
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-4.png#P13;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-f.png).
- The poster deck adds that the regression was tested over 200 trials with 189 successful shots and
  11 misses, and prints a correlation coefficient of 0.9880
  (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-10).
- Components: an adjustable hood that changes launch angle; a 1:1 gear-ratio dual-servo turret for
  near-instant aiming; a turret that lets the robot shoot from anywhere on the field; a weighted
  flywheel adding inertia; and an artifact blocker allowing a 150 ms three-ball unload
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-4.png#P13).
- The adjustable hood uses an 8-bearing system so it adjusts without sacrificing stability, giving
  zero backlash and near-instantaneous aiming
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-4.png#P13;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-16).
- V1 → V2: the dual-rail hood caused excess friction and HTD5 belts were inefficient; V2 uses a
  smooth hood that increases launch speed, a servo turret improved with metal gears, and GT2
  pulleys for higher efficiency
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-4.png#P13;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-16).

### 5.8 Software: teleop, autonomous, localization

All software facts below come from team-authored pages that name no student
(content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-5.png#P14).

**Architecture**

- The software is built on a **Task System**: a task is a single discrete robot action, layered
  vertically or horizontally to complete an operation. The team credits this modularity with a 10×
  faster code process (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-d.png;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-2).
- The stated control goal is to take full advantage of the robot's mechanical capabilities, with
  requirements of full sorting and artifact detection, automatic aiming, rapid fire and automatic
  localisation; the solution is modular code that uses all sensor data
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-5.png#P14).

**Sensing and localization**

- Sensors: two 4-bar odometry pods (dead wheels with rotary encoders) recording global coordinate
  position every 10 ms; a Limelight 3A computer-vision camera reading AprilTags; an IMU; motor
  encoders; transfer-motor current sensing; and colour sensors for sorting
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-5.png#P14;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-10).
- Localization is a 4-step pipeline: collect odometry, Limelight and IMU data, then merge with a
  **Kalman filter** — rotation from Limelight + IMU, position from odometry + Limelight
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-5.png#P14;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-d.png).
- The Limelight also enables sorting via an Obelisk scan of the game's AprilTag element
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-5.png#P14).
- Reported reliability: 98% AprilTag lock, 100% odometry availability, path deviation reduced to
  ±0.2 cm, turret correction every 50 ms
  (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-10).

**Launcher and intake software**

- A velocity PID controller uses launcher-wheel encoder feedback for consistent firing; distance to
  the goal comes from the fused odometry / IMU / Limelight estimate; a linear regression predicts
  launch power at any distance; and the turret auto-aims on the fused data
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-5.png#P14).
- The robot runs multiple PID controllers, offering adaptive adjustments, custom tuning, easy
  iteration, and storage inside a task
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-6.png#P15;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-d.png).
- In autonomous the modular approach allows a separate sequence for each artifact pattern; in
  TeleOp, colour sensors detect ball location and colour and indicate it to the driver via LEDs,
  and modular staging/fire sequences the rapid-fire sorting
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-5.png#P14).
- Transfer software uses task modularity to calculate the correct order of movements so balls leave
  the intake in the right order; drivetrain software drives until a point is reached, so
  interference cannot derail the autonomous sequence
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-5.png#P14).

**Driver feedback and controls**

- LED strips on the back of the robot show the driver how many artifacts it holds; artifact colour
  and location are determined by 5 colour sensors
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-6.png#P15;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-d.png).
- The driver button map on a PS4-style controller: flush out artifacts; reset odometry; load
  artifacts into transfer; launch/unload artifacts sequence; movement on the left stick; rotation on
  the right stick (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-6.png#P15).
- The team uses a non-linear joystick curve — printed as a cubic x³ = y on the engineering
  portfolio — in place of the linear curve most teams use, for precise low-speed driving
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-6.png#P15).
- The robot is single-driver (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-10).

**PEREGRINE — custom autonomous pathing**

- PEREGRINE is a custom, modular autonomous pathing software the team developed alongside a Simons
  Foundation professor, using what the team calls "Infinite Variable Calculus" to create optimal
  paths (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-6.png#P15;
  content/source/robotics/posters/poster-d.png).
- Its task system stores robot actions as separate classes — "tasks" — layered into sequences; the
  team credits this modularity with 10-minute rapid auton creation
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-6.png#P15).
- The corresponding Connect project describes weekly collaboration with an applied-mathematics
  expert on Calculus of Variations, producing a real-time algorithm that recalculates optimal paths
  if the robot is bumped during autonomous; the slide says the research will be published and
  released open-source (content/source/robotics/championships-presentation/slide-a.png).
- Cooperative autonomous: because alliance autons often clashed, the modular code plus PEREGRINE
  lets the team adapt strategy between matches to different partners, for an average increase of
  **+9 artifacts** scored during auton; the highest paired auton is **27 artifacts**
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-6.png#P15).
- The autonomous period is 30 seconds, and end-state data is saved for a seamless TeleOp transition
  (content/source/robotics/tritonics-portfolio/nyc-champs-portfolio-6.png#P15).
- Control iterations: V1 hardcoded paths, no localization, no auto-aim; V2 PID straight-line
  correction, sensor integration, partial auto-aim; V3 AprilTag localization, state carryover,
  optimized velocity recovery. Localization error and RPM variance each fell 30%
  (content/source/robotics/posters/poster-deck/text.md#page-10).
